01 / OUR SCIENCE

Science goals and
observational targets

K-DRIFT studies the distribution and structure of objects fainter than the night-sky background. To observe them, we develop optics that suppress scattered light, observing strategies suited to a wide field of view, and precise background calibration methods. We are also extending this work to test dark matter models, including self-interacting dark matter (SIDM).

03 / THE TELESCOPE

Unobscured optics for
faint, extended sources

K-DRIFT uses an unobscured three-mirror system with freeform optical surfaces. The design provides a wide field of view while reducing diffraction and scattered light associated with a central obstruction. The optics were designed, fabricated, aligned, and evaluated by researchers in Korea.

05 / RESEARCH IN DEPTH

Low surface brightness
and galaxy evolution

Stars and gas in galaxy outskirts, ultra-diffuse galaxies, and intracluster light allow us to study how galaxies gain mass and respond to their environments. K-DRIFT measures their distributions and structures for comparison with numerical simulations and observations at other wavelengths.

Science themes 01Dark matter · SIDM 02Methods 03Results 04Survey plans 05Papers & media 06

Surface brightness and detection limits

Surface brightness is the brightness per unit angular area on the sky. Even when two objects have the same total flux, the more extended object has a weaker signal per unit area and is harder to detect. In low surface brightness measurements, scattered light and errors in background calibration can be confused with astrophysical signals.

K-DRIFT observes a target and its surrounding sky within the same wide field. We aim to reduce background calibration errors through optics that suppress scattered light and an observing strategy that combines pointing offsets with camera rotation. Interview with the PI (Korean) ↗

SCIENCE THEMES Research topics

Four Science Theme papers describe our research on galaxies, galaxy clusters, dust and gas, and numerical simulations. A study of the optical colors of Galactic cirrus provides a specific example of how we distinguish signals in faint imaging.

01 / GALAXY ASSEMBLY

Galaxy mergers and stellar mass growth

Stellar streams, tidal tails, shells, and stellar halos retain evidence of interactions and mergers. We identify faint structures around nearby galaxies, measure their morphology and surface brightness, and study how stars accreted from other galaxies contribute to galaxy growth.

Targets · Tidal features, stellar halos, and the outskirts of nearby galaxies

Byun et al. 2026 · Galaxy science ↗
02 / GALAXY CLUSTERS

Intracluster light and cluster environments

We investigate cluster assembly through intracluster light—emission from stars not bound to individual galaxies—and the outskirts of central galaxies. Observations of ultra-diffuse galaxies and tidal features help us study environmental effects on galaxies. Comparing the spatial distributions of intracluster light and dark matter is a key direction linking multiwavelength data with simulations.

Targets · Intracluster light, central galaxies, ultra-diffuse galaxies, and tidal features

Yoo et al. 2026 · Galaxy cluster science ↗
03 / STAR FORMATION

Star formation in galaxy outskirts

Galaxy outskirts offer a way to study how star formation continues where gas surface densities are low. Combining low surface brightness imaging with surveys at other wavelengths allows us to map star formation and examine its connection to the growth of galactic disks.

Research direction · Star formation at low density and comparison across wavelengths

Byun et al. 2026 · Galaxy science ↗
04 / GALACTIC CIRRUS

Cirrus colors and dust scattering

Galactic cirrus, starlight scattered by dust in the Milky Way, can overlap faint structures in other galaxies. The Stripe 82 study compared observed optical colors of cirrus with dust-scattering radiative transfer models. These colors can help distinguish extragalactic emission and constrain the interstellar radiation field and dust-scattering properties.

Methods · Optical colors, radiative transfer, and scattering by interstellar dust

Seon et al. 2025 · Cirrus in Stripe 82 ↗
05 / CIRCUMGALACTIC MEDIUM

Dust and gas around galaxies

Proposed studies would probe dust in galactic halos and the circumgalactic medium through the reddening of background sources. Observations of Hα emission are intended to investigate star formation, galactic winds, and gas tails produced by ram-pressure stripping in clusters.

Research direction · Dust reddening, Hα emission, galactic winds, and gas stripping

Seon et al. 2026 · Dust and gas science ↗
06 / THEORY & SIMULATION

Galaxy replacement and mock observations

The Galaxy Replacement Technique (GRT) replaces low-resolution dark matter halos in cosmological simulations with high-resolution galaxy models. It follows the gravitational evolution of stars and dark matter to trace tidal features and intracluster light, helping interpret observations. It is distinct from models that directly simulate gas hydrodynamics and star formation.

Methods · Gravitational evolution of stars and comparison with faint structures

Chun et al. 2026 · Theoretical framework ↗
06 / DARK MATTER RESEARCH

Ultra-low surface brightness
and dark matter

The structure and evolution of galaxies are closely linked to the distribution of dark matter. K-DRIFT aims to test predictions of dark matter models by observing faint structures on scales from dwarf galaxies to galaxy clusters and comparing them with numerical simulations.

SELF-INTERACTING DARK MATTER

Self-interacting dark matter
SIDM

SIDM models allow dark matter particles to scatter off one another. These interactions can affect the dark matter density distribution and a galaxy’s response to tidal forces. We examine potentially observable differences from the standard cold dark matter (CDM) model, in which particle collisions are neglected.

Ultra-low surface brightness observations are needed to study stars stripped from galaxy outskirts and faint, extended galaxies. Measuring their numbers, sizes, and morphologies across different environments allows a more systematic comparison of the effects of galaxy assembly history and dark matter physics.

01 / DWARF & DIFFUSE GALAXIES

Dwarf and
ultra-diffuse galaxies

Changes in the central dark matter density can alter how galaxies lose mass through tidal interactions. Comparing galaxy sizes, surface brightness distributions, and environments allows us to investigate the structural changes and survival rates predicted by SIDM.

Observables
Size · Surface brightness distribution · Abundance by environment
Yang et al. 2020 · SIDM simulations ↗
02 / STELLAR HALOS & STREAMS

Stellar halos and
tidal features

Stellar streams, tidal tails, and shells reflect a galaxy’s gravitational potential and interaction history. Comparing their frequency and morphology with simulations helps us examine how both the dark matter distribution and merger history affect the observations.

Observables
Frequency of tidal features · Morphology · Outer light profiles
Chun et al. 2026 · Observations and simulations ↗
03 / INTRACLUSTER LIGHT

Intracluster light and
mass distributions

We compare how well central galaxies and intracluster light trace the dark matter distribution in different models. The observational approach is to combine faint starlight maps with total mass distributions inferred from gravitational lensing and hot-gas distributions measured in X-rays.

Observables
Light distribution · Morphology · Correspondence with mass maps
Yoo et al. 2026 · ICL and SIDM ↗

Interpreting observations with models

A single faint galaxy or tidal feature cannot uniquely identify SIDM. Energy released by star formation and the motion of gas can also change the central dark matter distribution. We aim to compare models using stellar and gas kinematics and gravitational lensing alongside brightness and morphology, while accounting for the conditions under which objects are detected. Yoo et al. · 2026

Comparing CDM and SIDM through intracluster light

This study compares two C-EAGLE galaxy clusters simulated with matched initial conditions. The spatial correspondence between the central galaxy plus intracluster light and the dark matter distribution is weaker in SIDM. The result motivates combining ultra-low surface brightness imaging with mass maps, and the paper identifies K-DRIFT as a potential facility for follow-up observations.

These are simulation results for a specific interaction strength. Tests across a wider range of clusters and models are needed before the models can be distinguished observationally.

07 / OBSERVATION & DATA REDUCTION

Optical design and
observing methods

Scattered-light suppression, observing strategy, and background calibration
are central to low surface brightness measurements.

01 · WIDE

Wide field of view

Extended sources and the surrounding sky needed for background estimation are observed together. G1 covers 4.43° × 4.43° in a single exposure.

02 · LOW SCATTER

Low scattered light

The system uses freeform mirrors without a central obstruction. Mirror fabrication and alignment errors, together with the baffling, affect image quality and scattered-light levels.

03 · FLAT

Uniform background

We correct for spatial variations in detector sensitivity and changes in sky brightness. Combining pointing offsets with camera rotation is being studied as a way to reduce flat-field calibration errors.

ROLLING DITHERING

Pointing offsets and rotation:
rolling dithering

Dithering changes the telescope pointing between exposures. Rolling dithering adds camera rotation, with an observing sequence designed to record the same objects and sky background at different positions and orientations on the detector.

Byun et al. (2025) compared the two approaches using simulated observations with spatially varying sky backgrounds. Adding rotation reduced errors in the dark-sky flat, the calibration image used to correct variations in detector sensitivity.

OBSERVATIONS & MOCK IMAGES

Matching models to observing conditions

Comparisons of faint galaxy and tidal-feature counts must account for objects missed by detection. We plan to inject simulated sources into images and recover them to measure completeness as a function of brightness and size. Mock observations will be matched in resolution, noise, and background processing to assess whether observational errors could be mistaken for differences between dark matter models.

Observing terms

Surface brightness and mag arcsec⁻² ＋

Surface brightness is brightness per unit angular area. In mag arcsec⁻², larger values mean fainter emission. Comparisons of surface brightness limits must specify the band, exposure time, measurement area, and detection threshold, such as 1σ or 3σ.

PSF and FWHM ＋

The point spread function (PSF) describes how light from a point source is distributed in an image. Its full width at half maximum (FWHM) is the width measured at half the peak intensity. Ultra-low surface brightness imaging requires assessment of scattered light in the PSF wings as well as the width of its core.

Dark-sky flat ＋

A dark-sky flat combines the night-sky background from multiple exposures after masking astronomical sources. It is used to correct spatial variations in detector response. Observing sequences and image-combination methods are designed to prevent sky structure from being imprinted on the flat.

11 / S-DRIFT

The future of
K-DRIFT

Space telescope concept study

S-DRIFT is a concept for extending the ultra-low surface brightness observing techniques developed through K-DRIFT into space. By combining near-ultraviolet and visible imaging, it aims to map faint stellar distributions and recent star formation for studies of galaxy evolution and dark matter.

Visible VIS
Faint structures in galaxy outskirts and intracluster light
Near-ultraviolet NUV
Recent star formation and its environmental dependence

Combining the two bands would help assess the effects of star formation and inform the interpretation of dark matter models. The concept study is developing the observing requirements and survey approach.
